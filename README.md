Introduction
Automated decision-making (ADM) is when a computer system makes a decision, or substantially and directly supports one, with little or no human involvement. It is used by governments for eligibility, debt calculations, fraud risk scoring and for support budgets. Changes to Australia's Privacy Act starting from 10 December 2026 have a similar test: a program that makes or significantly contributes to a decision that may significantly impact a person's rights or interests (JWS 2025; IDM 2026). 

It’s important to me as an ICT professional because it is the people who have the least power to challenge the ADM who are the ones who have to live with this decision. The ACS Code of Professional Conduct puts the public interest first (ACS 2021). Kantianism asks the question: are humans treated as ends in themselves, or just as data? And virtue ethics asks: what does it mean to be honest and courageous, when the system feels biased, but still works? (Quinn 2020) Prior to this unit, I believed computer decisions were more objective than human decisions and I thought Robodebt was a one-off bad algorithm. Now I see ADM as having the values, shortcuts and blind spots of the people, data and policies behind it, and I no longer think "a human is involved" is sufficient.





Artefact 1: Attorney-General's Department Consultation Paper on ADM by Government (Australia, 2024)



Summary of the Artefact

The Attorney-General's Department (2024) has released a consultation paper examining how the law should be changed to address automated decision making by Government. It addresses the Robodebt Royal Commission Recommendations 17.1 and 17.2, which set out the need for a consistent legal framework and a body to monitor and audit ADM. The paper covers the issue of transparency regarding the use of ADM, how decisions are made and the giving of statements of reasons.

Giving reasons and a pathway to review gives respect to the rational agent (Kantianism, Quinn 2020) and respects the public interest (ACS 2021).

Artefact 5: Amnesty International, Coded Injustice Report on Denmark's Welfare System (Denmark, November 2024)




Summary of the Artefact
Denmark's welfare authority, Udbetaling Danmark, utilizes AI and machine-learning tools to flag people for benefit fraud investigations, according to the report of Amnesty International (2024). It claims that this is dependent on “mass data collection” and can lead to the exclusion of disabled people, low income individuals, migrants, refugees and marginalised racial groups. Amnesty looked at four of the models and urged transparency and an end to proxy information like "foreign affiliation". Fortune (2024) reveals that approximately 90 per cent of opens in one algorithm were not fraudulent.



Justification for Choosing this Artefact
This artefact is my primary international case study because it demonstrates risks of Robodebt outside of Australia, in a country that is frequently considered a model welfare state. It taught me about the proxy discrimination, where protected attributes are not used directly, but can be recreated through a proxy, like place of birth, that is the bias problem in my Question 1 scenario. It involves the ACS Code on public interest and fairness (ACS 2021) and Kantian concept of respect for persons (Quinn 2020). Compared with many other countries with similar human rights regimes, Australia's human rights protections are not as strong, so transparency and review rights are even more significant in Australia (Huggins 2025).




Artefact 6: The Guardian, Bias Found in the UK DWP's AI Fraud-Detection System (United Kingdom, December 2024)




Summary of the Artefact
In The Guardian, Booth (2024) revealed that under freedom of information, a Department for Work and Pensions internal fairness analysis revealed a statistically significant outcome disparity in a machine-learning tool it uses to decide whether or not to review advance claims for Universal Credit for fraud. The differences in terms of age, disability, marital status and nationality. The decision was made by a human being and the analysis did not take into account certain aspects like race, sex or religion.



Justification for Choosing this Artefact
I selected this artefact as it has a very similar scenario to my Question 1 artefact, a system which fails internal checks but exhibits demographic bias. It shakes up the notion that "a human in the loop" fixes the issue as a human can also end up with the algorithm's bias if they review flagged cases. The ACS Code calls on me to bring up matters of harm to the public (ACS 2021), and virtue ethics suggests that my duty in this matter is to be courageous and honest (Quinn 2020). For a system I was testing, if I were to see anything like this, I would report it and request more bias testing.

Overall Reflection
This e-portfolio enabled me to gain insight into automated decision making in government systems. I learned that ADM is not only a technical issue. It is influenced by law and policy, by design, budget decisions, as well as by the people it impacts, who are often the least likely to contest it. The Australian artifacts demonstrated the impact of Robodebt for some to reform proposals that remain unfinished, and that ADM is now entering into other fields such as the NDIS. The Denmark and UK artefacts exhibited a similar pattern internationally: fraud control objectives, sub-optimal and biased models, limited transparency and reduced rights of review, even when the final decision is made by a human. As an ICT professional I will inquire early on whether a system is tested for bias, whether people whose fate is influenced by the system can understand and challenge the decisions, and whether a human can truly change its outcome. I will record and be concerned about "it meets the specification" rather than "it is fair". My aspiration is to create technology that treats people as the end rather than the means to a target.
